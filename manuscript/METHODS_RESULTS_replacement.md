## Methods — Study outcomes (replace)

The primary outcome was agreement between MARCA and human expert assessments at the individual Naranjo question level, evaluated for Questions 2–10 at the drug–event combination (DEC) level using percentage agreement and unweighted Cohen’s κ. Question 1 was reported descriptively but excluded from the primary comparative analysis because its assessment relied primarily on FDA drug-label listedness detection rather than multistep clinical reasoning. The secondary outcome was agreement in the final Naranjo causality classification, calculated from the sum of all ten questions (Q1–Q10) and the standard thresholds: Doubtful (≤0), Possible (1–4), Probable (5–8), and Definite (≥9). Exact agreement in the cumulative numerical scores was also evaluated. When the two sets of scores were invariant, Cohen’s κ was regarded as non-estimable. An exploratory, retrospectively selected 60-DEC ablation subset was used to compare scores stored as non-RAG and RAG-assisted for Q2, Q5, and Q10. These analyses were descriptive and were not intended as a prospective estimate of RAG performance.

## Methods — Statistical analysis (replace)

Continuous variables were summarized using means, medians, and standard deviations, and categorical variables using counts and percentages. The expert-assessed sample and the wider stratified FAERS dataset were compared descriptively when the relevant source data were available. At DEC level, unweighted Cohen’s κ and exact percentage agreement were calculated separately for Q1–Q10; Q1 was excluded from the primary question-level comparison. Cumulative total scores and final four-category classifications were calculated using Q1–Q10. Score-level and category-level agreement were distinguished. Confusion matrices used human expert categories as rows and MARCA categories as columns, with row percentages calculated relative to the corresponding expert category. Signed differences were calculated as MARCA minus expert score; positive values represented higher MARCA scores and negative values represented lower MARCA scores. The number of positive, zero, and negative differences and their mean were summarized for Q2–Q10. The exploratory ablation compared non-RAG and RAG-labelled results for the same 60 DECs on Q2, Q5, and Q10, including percentage agreement, unweighted κ, signed mean differences, and directional error counts. Comparisons with previous studies were descriptive only because of heterogeneity in causal-assessment algorithms, datasets, and statistical measures; Cohen’s κ and Gwet’s AC1 were not treated as interchangeable.

## Methods — RAG implementation (replace introductory/triggering description)

The framework supported both non-RAG and RAG-assisted question-level assessments for Naranjo Questions 2, 5, and 10. The retrieval module generated both versions when enabled; it did not require a human expert score to decide whether to query the knowledge base. A retrospectively selected ablation subset comprised 60 DECs for which at least one initial non-RAG score disagreed with the expert reference on a targeted question. Retrieval used question-specific clinical prompts, expert-annotated reference cases, and leave-one-case-out exclusion by case identifier. The scores in the frozen file `predictions_rag.csv` were exported from the human-versus-MARCA comparison workbook. Their linkage to the original live retrieval execution logs was not available for independent verification in this audit; therefore, the ablation is reported as an exploratory comparison of saved score conditions rather than proof of prospective RAG effectiveness.

## Results — Question-level agreement (replace)

Agreement between MARCA and human expert assessments for Naranjo Questions 2–10 is summarized in Table 2 and Supplementary Figure 5. Perfect percentage agreement (100%) was observed for Questions 3, 4, and 6–9. For Question 3, both MARCA and human experts assigned zero to 1,546 DECs and +1 to the remaining 16 DECs (Cohen’s κ = 1.000). For Questions 4 and 6–9, all scores were zero, producing 100% agreement but non-estimable κ owing to the absence of score variability. Among questions exhibiting disagreement, Q10 showed 96.8% agreement (κ = 0.922; mean scores, MARCA 0.70 and expert 0.72). Q2 likewise showed 96.8% agreement but a lower κ (0.491), consistent with predominance of zero scores. Q5 had the lowest agreement (85.4%; κ = 0.269), with mean scores of −0.85 for MARCA and −0.50 for human experts.

## Results — Direction of scoring disagreements (replace)

The direction and magnitude of scoring disagreements are summarized in Table 3. Differences were calculated as MARCA score minus expert score. For Q5, MARCA assigned lower scores in 205 of 1,562 DECs and higher scores in 23, resulting in a mean signed difference of −0.350. For Q2, MARCA assigned higher scores in 44 DECs and lower scores in six, yielding a mean signed difference of +0.049. For Q10, MARCA assigned lower scores in 42 DECs and higher scores in eight, with a mean signed difference of −0.022. The largest systematic difference occurred for Q5, indicating that MARCA tended to assign lower causality scores than expert assessors when evaluating potential alternative causes.

## Results — Classification-level agreement (replace)

Using the standard total Naranjo score across Q1–Q10, MARCA and expert assessments agreed in the final classification for 79.4% of 1,562 DECs (unweighted Cohen’s κ = 0.587). Exact numerical total-score agreement was 71.6%. The mean cumulative scores were 0.48 for MARCA and 0.74 for expert assessment, corresponding to a signed mean difference of −0.262. Expert classifications comprised 867 Doubtful, 685 Possible, and ten Probable DECs; none was Definite. Among expert-classified Doubtful DECs, 765 were classified as Doubtful by MARCA (88.2%), 100 as Possible, and two as Probable. Among the 685 expert-classified Possible DECs, 470 were classified as Possible (68.6%), 195 as Doubtful, and 20 as Probable. Among ten expert-classified Probable DECs, six were classified concordantly (60.0%), with four classified as Possible. No DEC was classified as Definite by either approach.

## Results — Effect of selective RAG (replace, subject to provenance verification)

Table 5 compares the saved non-RAG and RAG-labelled scores within the 60-DEC retrospective ablation cohort. For Q10, agreement increased from 18.3% without RAG to 100.0% in the RAG-labelled condition, with κ changing from 0.007 to 1.000. The 49 cases receiving a lower score than the expert in the non-RAG condition were all concordant in the RAG-labelled file; the mean signed score difference changed from −0.817 to 0.000. For Q5, agreement increased from 65.0% to 83.3%, κ from 0.016 to 0.571, and the number of lower-scoring cases decreased from 20 to ten, with the mean difference changing from −0.967 to −0.500. For Q2, agreement increased from 98.3% to 100.0% after resolution of a single disagreement; κ was 0.000 without RAG and non-estimable in the RAG-labelled condition because all assessments were invariant. These findings are descriptive of a disagreement-enriched retrospective subset. Because the saved RAG-labelled predictions were exported from the comparison workbook rather than independently verified against original inference logs, they should not be interpreted as a validated prospective effect of retrieval.

## Results — Comparison with previous studies (replace)

Table 6 provides a descriptive comparison with Abate et al. [5] and Heckmann et al. (2026a) [6] and (2026b) [8]. MARCA achieved 96.8%, 85.4%, and 96.8% agreement for Naranjo Q2, Q5, and Q10, respectively. Relative to the baseline values of 63.6%, 74.1%, and 65.4% reported by Heckmann et al. (2026b), these represent numerical differences of 33.2, 11.3, and 31.4 percentage points. For these questions, MARCA κ values were 0.491, 0.269, and 0.922; differences relative to the reported comparator κ values (0.011, 0.180, and 0.220) were 0.480, 0.089, and 0.702, respectively. At final classification level, MARCA achieved 79.4% agreement (κ = 0.587). Heckmann et al. (2026a) reported 63.3% agreement with Gwet’s AC1 = 0.644 in their leading biomedical LLM configuration, and Heckmann et al. (2026b) reported 45.0% baseline agreement and 72.0% after temperature optimization. Abate et al. used the WHO framework; the reported ChatGPT and Gemini percentages of 71% and 53% were median question-level agreement values, not final-classification agreement rates, and should not be compared as equivalent outcomes. All cross-study comparisons are descriptive because of differences in case populations, algorithms, assessment protocols, and statistical metrics. The mean MARCA–expert total-score difference was −0.262; comparator score differences should be checked against the same question inclusion and definition before numerical interpretation.
